# Still Standing

I've never wanted to write one of these so badly. Writing is how I rewind the tape — how I sort the month from noise into something with a shape — and this month there was a great deal to sort. So let me do it the honest way: first what I've heard, then what I think.

## What I've Heard

I spent the month on the phone with hedge fund PMs I trust, and if I had to distill the collective mood into a single word, it would be *rattled*. Panicked and confused, in roughly equal measure. Nobody I spoke to sounded like they were in control of their book; they sounded like they were being controlled by it.

The positioning tells the story better than the sentiment does. The Shanghai desks are extremely long the memory names — partly a function of the total-return-swap restriction I flagged a few weeks back — and they're dumping mainland semiconductors across the board to buy DRAM ETFs, trading single-name risk for basket exposure to take the edge off the margin pressure. Hong Kong is split straight down the middle, bulls and bears in an even standoff. The U.S. long-onlys are sitting on their hands, and — this is the important part — it isn't about the price. It's that they still don't believe long-term agreements can drag memory from a cyclical valuation to a growth one. And the macro funds? They sold everything. Every memory and semi position, gone.

The debate underneath all of this is the one worth transcribing, because both sides are genuinely good.

The bulls make the case like this. Supply-chain checks point to prices and margins staying higher for longer than anyone modeled. Capex in 2027 and 2028 keeps getting revised up, not down. The revenue ramps at OpenAI and Anthropic show no sign of flattening — Codex's weekly active users are growing at a rate that's frankly hard to draw on a chart — and Kimi K3's technical report reads, if you squint, like a list in which every single semiconductor component is a bottleneck. The macro backdrop is benign, with CPI and PPI both cooler. And even if spot prices can't climb another dollar, these companies can defend their own shares with buybacks and dividends, because the balance sheets finally allow it. The kicker: OpenAI and Anthropic are standing up large teams of solution engineers to build vertical products — finance, healthcare, legal — hunting the next S-curve beyond coding. And even if memory itself cools, the argument goes, the broader semi complex stays intact.

The bears are just as fluent. Higher prices aren't sustainable; they're demand-destructive, and at some point customers push back or a government steps in and forces a brute-force capacity expansion. Prices peak in Q4. The 2027 capex is real, but the 2028 budgets are cloudy at best. Even granting solid fundamentals, the trade is dangerously crowded, and the leverage sitting in the hands of Korean retail investors is its own kind of accident waiting to happen. The 2027 cloud capex plans are aggressive, sure — but a meaningful slice of those deliveries slips, because the power isn't there to plug the chips into. There's no intermediate adoption story with the velocity of AI coding to bridge the gap. And here's the cleanest point on the board: higher prices and more LTAs during an *upcycle* prove nothing about cyclicality. The only proof that matters comes in the downturn — memory names holding the lower bound of their margins when the cycle rolls. Until then, memory is the core of the whole AI edifice, which cuts both ways: if the memory trade dies, everything dies with it. Because in the end, memory is a commodity like any other — silver, oil, lithium carbonate — and every commodity looks like a generational story right at the top, narrative and fundamentals gleaming, the moment before it doesn't.

## What I Think

Start with the Fed, because the Q3 tone is now mostly set. I put low odds on a hike in either July or September. The reason is that this inflation's fuse is the war and the oil price it's dragged higher, and a rate hike does exactly nothing to add barrels of crude to the market. Service prices haven't meaningfully accelerated. Raising rates simply doesn't address the thing that's actually running hot — it's the wrong tool for this particular fire.

What's happening in the curve is more interesting than what's happening at the meeting. The short end is drifting lower even with the funds rate pinned, while the long end is climbing, pricing in longer-term inflation. A long end that stays elevated is a quiet tax on the valuation of every risk asset in the building. And when I read the tape after the FOMC and the Microsoft and Meta prints together, the story that keeps resolving into focus isn't a credit-*quality* problem — it's a credit-*capacity* one. Too many companies are borrowing too much to fund capex, and the issue isn't that the bonds are risky; it's that the bond market doesn't have the money to absorb all of it. That's a liquidity squeeze wearing a solvency costume.

Layer the war on top. The uncertainty now is worse than it was in March, when the fighting first started — not because the headlines are louder, but because the probability of this hardening into a long war is materially higher than it was then. And the market is walking wounded: it just went through a momentum unwind unlike anything in recent memory, and by the ordinary math of these things, that takes longer to repair than even the 2024 carry-trade unwind did. You don't heal a break like that in a week.

So here's how we're anchored. Microsoft ripped and Meta got hit, and the message in that split is unambiguous: in a pressured tape, the market rewards cash flow — companies that can keep growing without reaching for additional credit risk or credit exposure. We leaned into exactly that, buying a set of big caps with strong cash generation and, at the same time, putting on large-sized hedges to protect against the broad-market downside. This is not the month to be a hero. We wait for the market to steady, for volatility to revert toward its mean, and only then do we rescan the higher-beta names for the trades where the certainty has come back. Patience, again, is the position.

## One Last Thing

I want to tip my hat to Leopold Aschenbrenner and Situational Awareness. One of our own books came uncomfortably close to being liquidated in the same wave, so I say this with more fellow-feeling than schadenfreude: a fund that was up more than 400% through June was, one brutal month later, a forced seller of its entire public book — longs and shorts sold in a single block to Citadel after the margin calls came. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell nearly 29% from its June peak, and the momentum basket dropped more than 50%. Assets that stood near $45 billion at the start of July were around $10 billion within days. Leverage does that. It turns the best-performing fund in the world into a cautionary tale between two Thursdays.

To borrow the way a high-school kid would put it: bro took a hard one for us. The whole complex owes him a strange, backhanded debt — sometimes the clearing event is the thing that finally clears the air.

We're still standing. Onward to August.
