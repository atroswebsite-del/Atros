# Pranks and Mary Jane

April will, in all likelihood, go down as our best-performing month of 2026 — a fact I note with the appropriate amount of superstition. Call it the month of transition. Somewhere between the first cup of coffee on April 1st and the closing print on the 30th, the book turned. We unwound the short positions we'd been carrying since January and crossed the floor to the bull side. The reasoning wasn't sentimental. Positive gamma had pushed into territory you couldn't politely call moderate, and the prime book was flattening the profits we'd accumulated on the short side faster than conviction alone could justify holding them. So we moved.

The data, in retrospect, walked through a minefield without setting anything off. ADP and nonfarm payrolls at the top of the month — clean. CPI — clean. Nothing that pulled the market off the narrative it had decided to tell itself. Today's PCE print is the one I'd flag as carrying a faint hum of latent risk, though I don't want to overstate it. As is so often the case lately, the trouble traces back to oil. The Fed held rates where everyone expected them to hold rates. Liquidity sits at a reasonable level — no flood, no tourniquet, just the kind of unremarkable plumbing that lets price discovery actually happen.

![](/blog/april-2026-illustration.png)

## May and market efficiency

Which is why I suspect May will be the month of market efficiency. Dealer gamma is sitting close to neutral, and when the cushion thins out, prices start doing the work they're supposed to do. I'd expect a correction in semiconductors and a rotation into other sectors — the kind of breadth event that tends to look obvious in hindsight and feel uncomfortable while it's happening. ATROS is anchored accordingly. We're long Chinese ADRs heading into the meeting between the two kings, and we're waiting, patiently, for the right entry to step back into semis. Patience here is the position.

## A closing note

A closing note, slightly out of register with the rest of this letter. I want to take a moment to express my appreciation for Jerome Powell. The man dragged this market — and by extension, all of us — out of COVID, out of more than one edgy situation, and out of a parade of administrative shit shows that would have broken a less steady chair. He is, in my view, the most underappreciated and underrated Fed chair of my career. Time, as it tends to, will tell.

Onward to May. — ATROS W.M.
