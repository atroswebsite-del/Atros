# Understanding What Option Order Flows Are and How To Trade Options?

What exactly are option order flows? And how do you trade them?

In short, option order flows are the number of shares in various options that are bought or sold over a particular period of time, and they can be used to predict the movements of individual stocks and indexes.

That’s why it’s important to understand how option order flows work if you want to trade options successfully.

![](/blog/5.jpg)

When you first look at an option order flow chart, it may appear to be nothing more than an indecipherable series of up and down arrows with letters on them.

With some practice, however, you can learn to interpret these charts and use the information they provide to make profitable trades in your brokerage account.

This article will explain what option order flows are and how you can use them to your advantage as an options trader.

## Introduction

Stock options are derivatives, which means they derive their value from an underlying asset.

That underlying asset is a stock. This makes option order flows similar to stock order flows. Understanding order flows has always been important for successful traders, as options flow uncovers large and unusual option trades to monitor what other traders and institutions are doing.

As such, knowing how option order flows influence option prices allows you to adjust your risk profile on certain positions and even get in before high-impact news hits.

## The Basics of Option Flow

It’s important for traders to understand that there are three different ways that an option order can fill:

1. Immediately

2. at some future time/date

3. not at all.

The second type of execution—to be filled at some future time/date—is referred to as an option flow. When a retail options trader is executing an option trade and has no idea if his/her order will go through, he/she is essentially entering a wait-and-see option trade.

Option flows allow market participants to take action on their directional bets without having to wait indefinitely for their trades to fill. Because options trade at such low volumes relative to stocks, sometimes an investor’s options simply don’t get enough demand from other investors in order to complete a trade immediately when it hits the tape.

## Types of Option Flow

There are two main types of option order flows: sell-side and buy-side.

Sell-side flow is when a market maker or broker receives a large number of orders from their clients, which causes them to increase or decrease their quotes on options.

Buy-side flow, on the other hand, is when one or more institutional investors (such as hedge funds) place large orders in a particular stock, causing market makers to change their option prices.

## How Many Coincidences Until Mathematically Impossible?

Using my analysis for SPY options as an example, I ran a calculation on options with less than one hour before expiration.

I compared open interest at that time against total options volume in that minute—as well as previous minutes—and calculated how many coincidences existed per 1 million options traded (since there are about 1 million shares of SPY in one contract).

I found that, on average, there were just over 20,000 coincidences out of a million contracts.

A coincidence is when an investor buys an expiring option then sells it shortly after; conversely, they could also sell an expiring option then buy it shortly after.

In either case, coincidences represent a situation where you’re able to predict with a reasonable degree of certainty that a stock will move by a set amount in a short time frame.

To determine whether it’s mathematically possible for coincidences to exist, I ran another calculation using just 1 million options traded—and found that there was only 1 coincidence out of 100 million options traded!

### Let’s get started with option order fills...

An option order fill (OPR) is an execution that occurs when a specific price is reached on a stock or index in relation to an options trade.

The way it works is that when you place an options trade, you can specify a limit price.

Which then triggers an OPR once that limit price is reached by way of your stock being at that level or higher.

## Who Uses OPR Fills?

When you place an order to buy a stock, you must decide whether you will sell that stock if your order isn’t filled.

If you don’t want it, why are you placing an order?

Enter OPR fills – what they are, who uses them, and how they can be used in your favor. The most important detail about OPR fills is understanding where they come from.

After all, there’s no point in being a professional if no one uses your services.

To get an idea of who uses OPR fills, you must first understand that OPR is a computer system used by investment banks around Wall Street.

When an investor places an order on his or her trading platform (typically through their broker), it is sent through several computers before it reaches its destination.

This information must then be validated, but it also goes out to various investment banks so they can determine if they want that same trade.

If you place a limit order for 100 shares of IBM at $100 per share, chances are there’s a potential buyer in line ahead of you with a limit order for 1,000 shares.

## Best Times to Use OprFlow: Save the Best for Last.

In any given week, some days are going to see much higher option order flow than others.

This creates a two-fold opportunity.

You can use OprFlow data to determine when to put in a new trade order.

Or you can see which option positions are getting closed out and decide if you want to jump in on that action by opening up your own trades accordingly.

If you’re looking for a good time to buy or sell options, pay attention to OprFlow data and avoid times when there is higher than average activity.

You’ll likely have much better luck at these calmer times of lower activity.

It will be easier for you to find solid opportunities at a relatively stable price. In addition, there is less chance of seeing a big change in an option’s price because there is less activity surrounding it.

This strategy works especially well on low-volume days or when options are moving towards their expiration date since OprFlow data tends to diminish during these times as well.
