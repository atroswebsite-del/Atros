# What Are Insider Trades And How Does This Info Help With Trading?

Insider trading is when someone with knowledge of important company information uses that knowledge to make money in their own investment accounts.

Insiders are able to do this because they have access to information that the general public does not have access to, like profit and loss statements, quarterly earnings, etc. The general public can only speculate as to how their investments are doing based on rumors, news stories, and other publicly available information.

This means that when an insider trades their company’s stock they have an unfair advantage over regular investors because they can see inside the company and know exactly what’s going on behind the scenes.

## What Are Insider Trades?

So, what are insider trades? Well, let’s start at ground zero.

The dictionary defines insider trading as the illegal buying or selling of a security by someone who has access to nonpublic information about that security.

Basically, if you have inside information (about anything) that could affect an investment you might make – then don’t trade on it.

## When is Inside Information Classified as an Insider Trade?

Insider information worth remembering:

If a company employee has confidential material nonpublic information, they have an obligation to keep that info secret.

As soon as they tell another person, whether that be via email or even at a cocktail party, it’s likely an insider trade has taken place.

It’s important to keep in mind that it isn’t just corporate employees who need to be on guard about insider trades. When directors, executive officers, major shareholders, or anyone else receives confidential information, they need to follow insider trading laws as well.

In fact, it’s illegal for them to trade on nonpublic material information even if they didn’t receive it directly from a company employee. (They could have received it indirectly through another individual.)

This is why many investors use calendars that show executives’ buys and sells along with those of corporate insiders so they can better predict when an unexpected event may occur.

## Is All Inside Information Illegal to Trade On?

Knowledge Made Public: An informed public on Insider trades is what they fear the most.

In most countries, there are no rules which prevent investors from trading on insider information.

The main reason for that is that it would be very hard to get rid of all insider trades.

Thinking logically, it's impossible to track every single person in a company that has some sort of access to inside information (Ie: everyone who might have seen something).

And without any proof that someone traded stocks based on inside information, why should anyone care?

Of course, there's still a huge problem when some people have an unfair advantage over others.

It basically destroys any faith in markets as a free, fair place to trade stocks.

That's why regulators around the world want to make sure that they can punish insider traders if possible.

## Who Are Insiders in the United States?


Insiders in the United States (United States) can be broadly classified into 2 types.

Those 2 categories are:

natural persons and legal entities (both corporations).

Legal entities consisting of officers, directors, significant shareholders, or partners that own 10% or more of a publicly-traded company’s outstanding equity securities fall under the insider category.

These insiders must file public reports disclosing their transactions in a company’s stock within 2 business days.

The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 mandates these disclosures.

These reports provide valuable insights into how different investors view a particular stock. This information can help you make better trading decisions. Insider transactions can also lead to changes in the price of securities.

Wealthy people, such as hedge fund managers, buy large stakes in companies.

They do this if they believe they're undervalued.

Then, they sell them when there is an expected increase in the company’s value.

However, their holdings may not reflect the economic reality of common investors.

Also, company insiders or employees usually have different information than other shareholders.
This gives them an edge over other market participants.

This information can prove to be valuable when predicting a company’s earnings report, pricing trends or stock buyback programs, etc.

So it is prudent to pay attention to these reports if you want to maximize your investment profits over time.

## Can I Get Caught for These Crimes?

Almost all insider trading laws apply to public companies (which just means any corporation that’s traded on a stock exchange).

But there is no legal definition of an insider.

Nor is there a clear-cut distinction between those who possess material non-public information.

Just because you work for a large corporation doesn’t mean you can use its secrets to make money in your personal investment account.

Rather, most insider trading laws center on people who have access to non-public information because they:

Are corporate insiders (i.e., top executives).
Work for an affiliated company (like a subsidiary or parent company).
Control 5% or more of that stock.
Knowingly provide false information about their employer or affiliate. They do so in order to take advantage of market fluctuations caused by new public knowledge.
